Pricing is where most Bangladeshi freelancers go wrong. They charge too little, attract terrible clients, burn out, and quit — all because nobody taught them how to price.

This guide gives you realistic rate ranges, pricing models, and a clear strategy to raise your prices over time.

Important note: These are observed market ranges, not guarantees. Your actual rates depend on your skill level, niche, client type, and negotiation.

Why Bangladeshi Freelancers Undercharge

Three reasons:

  1. Comparing to local salaries. "If a job pays 30,000 BDT/month, charging $500 for a project feels like too much." Wrong comparison. International clients pay international rates. A US business paying $500 for a logo is getting a deal — the same work costs them $2,000+ locally.
    1. Fear of losing the client. Beginners think low prices win work. They win the worst work — clients who demand the most and pay the least.
      1. No pricing framework. Most freelancers guess. This guide replaces guessing with a system.

        2. Pricing Models: Which One to Use

        1. Hourly Pricing

        You charge per hour worked.

        Best for: Ongoing work, consulting, tasks with unclear scope.

        How to set your rate: Start with your target monthly income, divide by billable hours.

        Example: Target $1,000/month ÷ 100 billable hours = $10/hour starting rate.

        Pros: Simple. Fair for both sides on open-ended work.

        Cons: Punishes efficiency (faster work = less pay). Clients may question your hours. Hard to scale income.

        2. Project-Based Pricing (Recommended for Most)

        You charge a fixed price for a defined deliverable.

        Best for: Design, writing, video editing — anything with a clear output.

        How to set your price: Estimate hours × your hourly rate + 20% buffer.

        Example: A logo takes 5 hours × $15/hour = $75 + buffer = $90 project price.

        Pros: Client knows the cost upfront. You earn more as you get faster. Easier to sell.

        Cons: Scope creep (client keeps adding requests). Fix this with a clear contract.

        3. Value-Based Pricing (Advanced)

        You charge based on the result you deliver, not the time you spend.

        Best for: Experienced freelancers in marketing, copywriting, development.

        Example: A landing page that generates $10,000 in sales is worth more than $200. You might charge $1,500 — still a great deal for the client.

        Pros: Highest earning potential. Aligns your pay with client results.

        Cons: Requires proof of past results. Harder to justify without a track record.

        4. Retainer Pricing (Best for Stability)

        Client pays a fixed monthly fee for ongoing work.

        Best for: Social media management, regular content, ongoing ad management.

        Example: $400/month to manage a client's Instagram (12 posts + stories + engagement).

        Pros: Predictable income. Deeper client relationships. Less time finding new clients.

        Cons: Requires trust. Client may expect unlimited work — define deliverables clearly.

        Our recommendation: Start with project-based pricing. Move to retainers once you have steady clients. Explore value-based pricing after 1+ years.

        The Pricing Ladder: When to Raise Prices

        Follow this timeline:

        Months 1–3: Entry pricing. Price at the low end of beginner rates. Your goal is reviews and experience, not maximum profit. Get 10+ completed projects.

        Months 4–6: First raise. Increase prices 25–50%. You now have reviews and proof. New clients pay the new rate. Existing good clients can stay at old rates temporarily.

        Months 7–12: Market rates. Price at intermediate levels. Be selective — say no to low-budget inquiries. Focus on clients who value quality.

        Year 2+: Premium positioning. Specialize in a niche. Charge experienced rates. Compete on expertise, not price.

        The rule: Every time your calendar fills up (you are turning down work), raise prices 20%. Repeat forever.

        How to Quote a Project

        Use this simple formula:

        1. Break down the work. List every deliverable. (Example: 1 logo, 3 revisions, source files.)
        2. Estimate your time. Be honest. Add 20% buffer for revisions and communication.
        3. Multiply by your rate. Hours × hourly rate = base price.
        4. Add project value. If the work is urgent, complex, or high-value to the client, add 10–30%.
        5. Present as a package. "Logo design package: $120 — includes 3 concepts, 2 revision rounds, all source files, delivered in 5 days."

          6. Never say: "My rate is $X/hour, how many hours do you think it takes?" This puts pricing control in the client's hands.

          Handling "That's Too Expensive"

          When a client says your price is too high, do NOT immediately discount. Instead:

          1. Ask what their budget is. "I understand. What budget did you have in mind for this?" Their answer tells you if they are serious or just bargain-hunting.
          2. Reduce scope, not price. "I can do the logo without the brand guidelines document for $80 instead of $120." You protect your rate while giving them an option.
          3. Walk away if needed. "It sounds like we're not the right fit on budget. No worries — I hope you find someone great!" Some of these clients come back later. Desperate discounting never earns respect.

            4. The 10% rule: If fewer than 10% of prospects say you are too expensive, your prices are too low.

            Pricing for Bangladeshi (Local) Clients

            Local clients pay less but offer advantages: bKash payments, no platform fees, easier communication, potential for long-term relationships.

            Typical local rates (as a rough guide, 30–50% below international):

            • Logo design: 2,000–8,000 BDT
            • Social media monthly management: 8,000–25,000 BDT
            • Website: 10,000–40,000 BDT
            • Video editing (per video): 1,500–8,000 BDT

            Tip: Quote local clients in BDT. It feels more natural and avoids exchange rate confusion.

            Avoid These Pricing Traps

            1. Charging per revision. Include 2–3 revision rounds in your price. Unlimited revisions = unlimited work.
            2. No advance payment. Always take 30–50% upfront for new clients. This filters out non-serious inquiries.
            3. Forgetting taxes and fees. If Fiverr takes 20%, your $100 gig pays you $80. Price accordingly.
            4. Comparing yourself to the cheapest seller. There is always someone cheaper. Compete on quality, speed, and communication instead.
            5. Never raising prices. If you charged the same rate for 2 years, you gave yourself a pay cut (inflation). Review prices every 6 months.

              6. Frequently Asked Questions

              Should I list my prices publicly?

              For packaged services (Fiverr gigs, website packages), yes — it filters out budget mismatches. For custom work (Upwork, direct clients), "starting from $X" works better than a fixed price list.

              How do I price something I've never done before?

              Estimate the hours generously, multiply by your rate, add 30% for the learning curve. Be transparent: "This is a new service for me, so I'm pricing it accessibly while I build experience in this area."

              Is it okay to charge different clients different prices?

              Yes. Pricing depends on project complexity, client budget, timeline, and relationship. A startup and a corporation should not pay the same. Just do not lie about it.

              When should I stop charging hourly?

              When you can accurately estimate project scope and your speed is above average. Project pricing rewards efficiency. Most experienced freelancers prefer it.
