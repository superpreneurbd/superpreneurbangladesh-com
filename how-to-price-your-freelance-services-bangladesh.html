Pricing is the hardest part of freelancing. Charge too little, and you work yourself to exhaustion for peanuts. Charge too much, and clients disappear. Most Bangladeshi freelancers default to charging too little — then wonder why they are burned out and broke.

This guide gives you practical pricing frameworks, not vague advice. You will learn how to set rates, when to raise them, and how to handle the "your price is too high" conversation.

Why Bangladeshi Freelancers Underprice

Before frameworks, understand the psychology:

  1. Taka-to-dollar confusion. $50 feels like "a lot" (it is 6,000+ taka), so freelancers hesitate to charge it — forgetting that international clients think in dollars, not taka.
    1. Comparison to local salaries. "I earned 30,000 taka/month at my job, so 50,000/month freelancing feels like a lot." But freelancing has no benefits, no paid leave, no job security, and inconsistent income. You need to earn more, not the same.
      1. Fear of losing the client. Beginners think any client is better than no client. This leads to accepting terrible rates, which leads to resentment and burnout.
        1. No pricing education. Nobody teaches pricing. Schools teach skills, not business.

          2. The fundamental reframe: You are not charging for your time. You are charging for the value you create and the problems you solve.

          Pricing Models: Which to Use

          Hourly Pricing

          How it works: You charge per hour worked.

          Pros: Simple, fair for undefined scope, easy to calculate.

          Cons: Punishes efficiency (faster work = less pay), clients worry about inflated hours, income ceiling.

          Best for: Consulting, ongoing maintenance, tasks with unclear scope.

          How to set your hourly rate:

          1. Calculate your target monthly income (e.g., 80,000 taka)
          2. Divide by billable hours (not all hours are billable — use 100 hours/month as realistic)
          3. 80,000 / 100 = 800 taka/hour minimum
          4. Add 30% buffer for taxes, dry spells, and non-billable time = ~1,000 taka/hour

            5. For international clients, convert to dollars. 1,000 taka is roughly $8–9. Most international clients expect to pay $15–50+/hour depending on skill and experience. Do not anchor to taka when pricing in dollars.

            Project-Based Pricing

            How it works: Fixed price for a defined deliverable.

            Pros: Rewards efficiency, clients know total cost upfront, higher earning potential.

            Cons: Scope creep risk, requires accurate estimation.

            Best for: Websites, designs, articles, videos — anything with clear deliverables.

            How to price a project:

            1. Estimate hours required (be honest, then add 25% buffer)
            2. Multiply by your hourly rate
            3. Add value premium (if the project generates significant value for the client, charge more than cost-plus)

              4. Example: A business website takes you 20 hours. Your rate is 1,000 taka/hour. Base: 20,000 taka. But this website will generate leads worth lakhs for the client. Project price: 35,000–50,000 taka. You are pricing value, not just hours.

              Retainer Pricing

              How it works: Monthly fixed fee for ongoing work.

              Pros: Predictable income, deeper client relationships, less time spent finding new clients.

              Cons: Requires trust, scope must be clearly defined.

              Best for: Social media management, SEO, ongoing content, maintenance.

              How to price: Monthly project rate x 0.8 (retainer discount for commitment), with clearly defined deliverables and revision limits.

              Value-Based Pricing (Advanced)

              How it works: Price based on the outcome for the client, not your time.

              Example: You write email sequences. One client's email list generates 500,000 taka/month in sales. Your emails increase conversion by 20% = 100,000 taka/month additional revenue. Charging 30,000 taka for this work is a bargain for the client.

              Best for: Experienced freelancers with proven results. Requires confidence and case studies.

              Start here when: You have 1+ years of experience and can point to specific results you have created.

              Pricing by Experience Level

              These are general frameworks, not fixed rules. Adjust for your skill, niche, and client type:

              Beginner (0–6 months, building portfolio)

              • Goal: Get clients and testimonials, not maximum income
              • Strategy: Price 20–30% below market rate, but never work for free (except 1–2 strategic portfolio pieces)
              • Focus: Over-deliver to earn reviews and referrals

              Intermediate (6–18 months, steady clients)

              • Goal: Sustainable income
              • Strategy: Price at market rate. Raise rates for new clients every 3–6 months.
              • Focus: Specialize in a niche to justify higher rates

              Advanced (18+ months, strong reputation)

              • Goal: Maximum value per hour
              • Strategy: Price above market. Be selective about clients. Use value-based pricing where possible.
              • Focus: Fewer clients, higher rates, deeper relationships

              The Art of Raising Prices

              Most freelancers never raise prices. Here is how to do it without losing clients:

              For New Clients

              Simply quote higher. New clients do not know your old rates. Every 3–6 months, increase your quotes by 10–20%.

              For Existing Clients

              1. Give notice. "Starting next month, my rate will be X" (30 days notice is fair).
              2. Explain briefly. "As my skills and demand have grown..." (one sentence, not a novel).
              3. Grandfather selectively. For your best long-term clients, offer a smaller increase or a transition period. For difficult clients, let the increase naturally filter them out.

                4. What happens: You will lose some clients. This is good. The clients you lose are usually the most demanding and lowest-paying. You replace them with better clients at higher rates.

                The Annual Review

                Once a year, review all client rates. Anyone below your current minimum gets a raise notice or gets replaced. This prevents the slow erosion where old clients pay 2023 rates in 2026.

                Handling "Your Price Is Too High"

                This objection will come. Here is how to handle it:

                Do not immediately discount. Discounting teaches clients that your first price was inflated.

                Instead, ask: "What budget did you have in mind?" Then:

                • If their budget is close: Offer to adjust scope (fewer deliverables, simpler version) to fit their budget. You protect your rate; they get what they can afford.
                • If their budget is far below: Politely decline. "I understand. Based on what you need, I do not think I am the right fit at that budget. I would rather be honest than deliver something rushed." This professionalism often brings them back later with a real budget.
                • If they want the same work for less: Hold firm. "My pricing reflects the quality and results I deliver. I would rather lose the project than deliver subpar work."

                Never say: "Okay, I can do it cheaper." This destroys your positioning permanently with that client.

                Pricing Psychology Tips

                1. Anchor high. Present your premium option first. It makes your standard option look reasonable.
                  1. Offer 3 tiers. Basic / Standard / Premium. Most clients choose the middle. The tiers guide them there.
                    1. Price in dollars for international clients. $500 sounds more standard than "60,000 taka" to a US client, even though they are roughly equivalent.
                      1. Avoid round numbers. 47,500 taka feels more calculated (and justified) than 50,000. For dollars: $487 beats $500 psychologically.
                        1. Charge for revisions separately. Include 2 revisions in your base price. Additional revisions cost extra. This prevents endless revision cycles.
                          1. Require deposits. 50% upfront for new clients, always. This filters serious clients from time-wasters and protects you from non-payment. Our payment dispute guide covers what to do when things go wrong.

                            2. Common Pricing Mistakes

                            | Mistake | Why It Hurts | Fix |

                            |---------|--------------|-----|

                            | Charging local rates to international clients | You leave massive money on the table | Research international rates for your skill; price in dollars |

                            | Not accounting for non-billable time | Your "hourly rate" covers only half your working hours | Calculate based on billable hours (50–60% of total) |

                            | Giving discounts without reducing scope | Clients learn to always negotiate | Discount = reduced scope, always |

                            | Pricing based on what you need (not value) | "I need 50k/month" is not a pricing strategy | Price based on market rates and value delivered |

                            | Never raising prices | Inflation and skill growth make old rates obsolete | Scheduled increases every 6 months minimum |

                            Frequently Asked Questions

                            How much should a beginner freelancer in Bangladesh charge?

                            There is no single number — it depends on your skill. As a framework: research what others with similar skills charge on Upwork/Fiverr, then price yourself in the lower-middle range while building reviews. Never compete solely on being cheapest — compete on reliability and communication.

                            Should I charge different rates for local vs international clients?

                            Yes. International clients typically pay 3–5x local rates for the same work. This is normal and reflects different markets, not exploitation. Price each market appropriately.

                            How do I know if my prices are too low?

                            Signs: you are fully booked but still struggling financially, clients never negotiate (they are getting a deal), you feel resentful about the work, or competitors with similar skills charge significantly more.

                            What about friends and family discounts?

                            Decide your policy in advance. Options: full price (cleanest), 20% discount (generous but bounded), or "I do not do discounted work, but I can refer you to someone affordable." Whatever you choose, apply it consistently to avoid awkwardness.

                            Should I list prices on my website/portfolio?

                            For project-based work with standard packages: yes, it filters inquiries. For custom work: list "starting from" prices. Transparency saves time for both sides. ## The Bottom Line Pricing is a skill, not a guess. Build it systematically: 1. Calculate your minimum viable rate (target income ÷ billable hours + buffer) 2. Choose the right model (hourly, project, retainer, or value-based) 3. Raise rates every 3–6 months for new clients 4. Never discount without reducing scope 5. Require deposits, always The freelancers who earn well are not necessarily the most skilled — they are the ones who learned to price their skills correctly.
