Most people who want to write an ebook never finish one. Not because they lack knowledge — because they lack a plan. They start writing chapter one, get stuck on the introduction, rewrite it twelve times, and quit by day four.

Writing an ebook in 30 days is absolutely possible. Thousands of first-time authors do it every month. The secret is not talent or inspiration. It is a simple system: one topic, one outline, a daily word target, and a rule against editing while you write.

This guide gives you that system, week by week.

Week 1: Pick Your Topic and Build the Outline (Days 1–7)

Everything starts with the right topic. The wrong topic will kill your ebook before you write a single chapter.

The 3 Rules of Topic Selection

1. Write what you know from experience. Your first ebook should come from something you have actually done. If you learned SEO by doing client work, write about SEO. If you grew a Facebook page to 50,000 followers, write about that. Readers pay for real experience, not researched theory.

2. Solve one specific problem. "How to make money online" is not a topic — it is a library. "How to get your first 10 freelancing clients on Upwork" is a topic. Narrow beats broad every time. A 60-page ebook that solves one problem sells better than a 200-page ebook that solves nothing.

3. Pick an audience you understand. Write for people like you were two years ago. You know exactly what they struggle with, what questions they ask, and what language they use. That makes writing ten times easier.

Build the Outline

Spend days 4–7 building a chapter outline. Aim for 8–12 chapters. Each chapter should answer one question your reader has.

A simple method: write down every question your target reader might ask about the topic. Group similar questions together. Each group becomes a chapter. Order them from beginner to advanced.

Example outline for "How to Start Freelancing in Bangladesh":

  1. Is freelancing right for you? (honest assessment)
  2. Choosing your first skill
  3. Setting up your profiles (Upwork, Fiverr)
  4. Writing proposals that get replies
  5. Your first 3 clients: how to land them
  6. Pricing your work
  7. Getting paid: Payoneer, Wise, bKash
  8. Taxes and legal basics
  9. Scaling from side income to full-time
  10. Common mistakes and how to avoid them

    11. Notice how each chapter is a question answered. That is all an outline needs to be.

    Week 2: Write the Messy First Draft (Days 8–16)

    This is where most people fail. Here is the rule that saves you: write forward, never backward.

    The Daily Word Target

    Set a target of 1,000 words per day. That sounds like a lot, but it is about 45 minutes of focused writing for most people. In 9 days, that is 9,000 words — a solid short ebook. Many nonfiction ebooks are only 10,000–15,000 words total.

    Write every day. Even on days you feel like your writing is terrible. Especially on those days.

    The "Ugly Draft" Rule

    Your first draft is allowed to be ugly. In fact, it should be. The goal of week 2 is words on the page, not beautiful sentences.

    Turn off your inner editor. Do not rewrite sentences. Do not research while writing — if you need a fact, type [CHECK THIS] and move on. Do not reorganize chapters. Just write the next sentence.

    Professional authors call this "shitty first drafts." It is the only way books get finished. Editing comes later.

    Where to Write

    Use Google Docs or Microsoft Word. Nothing fancy. Do not buy special writing software for your first ebook — it is a distraction disguised as preparation.

    Write in the same place at the same time every day. Morning works best for most people because willpower is highest. Even 45 minutes before work adds up fast.

    Week 3: Finish the Draft and Start Editing (Days 17–23)

    Finish Strong

    By day 17, you should have 60–70% of the draft done. The finish line is close, and this is where motivation dips. Push through. Write the remaining chapters even if they feel rough.

    On day 20 (or whenever you type the last word), celebrate. You have done what 90% of aspiring authors never do: finished a draft.

    The First Edit: Structure

    Take one full day off after finishing. Do not look at the manuscript. Then come back and read it as a reader, not a writer.

    Ask these questions:

    • Does the order of chapters make sense?
    • Is anything missing? (A question your reader would ask that you did not answer?)
    • Is anything repeated?
    • Does each chapter deliver on its promise?

    Move chapters around. Cut what does not serve the reader. Add short sections where gaps exist. This structural edit is the most important edit you will do.

    The Second Edit: Clarity

    Now go chapter by chapter and fix the writing itself:

    • Cut filler words and repeated phrases
    • Break long paragraphs into shorter ones
    • Replace vague statements with specific examples
    • Make sure every piece of advice is actionable

    Read your work out loud. If a sentence sounds awkward when spoken, rewrite it. This one trick improves writing more than any grammar tool.

    Week 4: Polish and Prepare for Publishing (Days 24–30)

    The Final Edit: Proofreading

    By now your ebook is structurally sound and clear. Week 4 is about polish:

    • Fix typos and grammar (Grammarly's free version helps)
    • Check all links and references
    • Make formatting consistent (headings, bullet points, spacing)
    • Write the introduction and conclusion last — now you know what the book actually says

    Write the Introduction Last

    This sounds backwards, but it works. You cannot write a good introduction until you know exactly what is in the book. Your introduction should:

    • Name the reader's problem (show you understand them)
    • Promise the transformation (what they will be able to do after reading)
    • Explain why you are qualified to teach this (briefly)
    • Tell them how to use the book

    The Conclusion and Call to Action

    End with a clear next step. What should the reader do after finishing? Give them one action to take immediately. If you have other products, a YouTube channel, or an email list, mention them here — but keep it to a short paragraph, not a sales pitch.

    Common Excuses (And Why They Are Wrong)

    "I am not an expert." You do not need to be the world's leading expert. You need to be one step ahead of your reader. If you figured out something that took you six months, someone starting today will gladly pay to learn it in a weekend.

    "Someone already wrote about this." Yes, and someone will write about it after you too. Readers choose books based on the author's voice, perspective, and relatability — not just the topic. Your experience is unique.

    "I do not have time." 45 minutes a day for 30 days. That is less time than most people spend scrolling social media. You have the time; the question is whether the ebook matters enough to claim it.

    "What if nobody buys it?" Then you have still gained something valuable: proof that you can finish a book, a writing sample, and an asset you can give away to build an audience. The first ebook is never just about sales.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How long should my first ebook be?

    Aim for 10,000–15,000 words (roughly 50–80 pages). That is long enough to deliver real value and short enough to actually finish. Longer is not better — focused is better.

    Do I need to be a good writer?

    No. You need to be clear. Short sentences, simple words, and real examples beat fancy writing every time. Most bestselling nonfiction ebooks are written in plain language.

    Should I write in English or Bangla?

    Write in the language your audience reads. For Bangladeshi readers learning practical skills, Bangla often works better because there is less competition. For international audiences, English opens a bigger market. Pick the one where you can write most naturally.

    What if I get stuck in the middle?

    Go back to your outline and write the easiest remaining chapter. Momentum matters more than order. You can also talk through the chapter out loud and record yourself, then transcribe it — speaking is often easier than writing.

    Do I need an ISBN or copyright registration?

    For your first ebook sold directly to readers, no. If you later publish on Amazon KDP, Amazon provides a free ISBN option.
